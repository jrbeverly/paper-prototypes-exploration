---
title: Cheap learning
---

**The assumption:** Building something that gets thrown away is a waste of time.

**The question:** What if the low cost of a prototype is not a compromise — but the whole point?

---

The value of a prototype lies in its low cost.

This sounds obvious, but it has a non-obvious consequence: the cheaper the prototype, the more freely you can experiment. When building something costs days of effort, you think carefully before starting. You build only what you are reasonably confident will work. You avoid dead ends.

When building something costs minutes, none of that restraint applies. Build ten variations. Try the weird idea. Pursue a path even if it probably will not work — because finding out *why* it does not work is itself valuable.

This is the economic argument for paper prototypes. Not that prototypes are good despite being cheap, but that they are good *because* they are cheap.

---

## Discarding a prototype is not failure

Teams sometimes resist prototyping because throwing work away feels wrong. But a discarded prototype is evidence of learning. It means you discovered something before investing in production code, where the cost of change would have been far higher.

The lower the cost of experimentation, the more ideas you can explore. And the more ideas you explore, the better your final understanding will be. The prototypes you discard are not waste — they are the price of the answer, and that price is almost always worth paying.

---

## AI changes the economics

AI-assisted development dramatically reduces the cost of producing exploratory software. A prototype that once took a day might now take an hour. One that took an hour might take minutes.

This means the optimal number of prototypes for any given problem has increased. Where it was once rational to build one careful prototype, it may now be rational to build five quick ones.

AI should increase the frequency of experimentation, not the length of specifications.
